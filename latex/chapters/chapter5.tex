\chapter{A New Monolithic Gas Detector}
\label{chap:chapter5}

The current Gas Pixel Detectors that are employed on board of IXPE do not match the requirements of the next generation of X-ray polarimeters, such as eXTP. The strictest requirements that have to be satisfied concern the detector stability over time, the possibility of detecting photons at a much higher rate than IXPE and reducing the spurious modulation, as eXTP will not be able to perform dithering. While progress in the development of a new ASIC to support higher rates has already been achieved with XPOL-III, as described in Chap. \ref{sec:xpol3}, solutions to improve detector stability and to have a more uniform response are currently under study.

Current GPDs employ a finely pixelated ASIC to reconstruct the two-dimensional photoelectron track produced after X-ray absorption. This is fundamental for a photoelectric polarimeter, and thanks to the $50~\mu\text{m}$ pixels, the GPD achieves high spatial accuracy, which allows to achieve a higher polarization sensitivity than Bragg crystal polarimeters.

As mentioned in Chap. \ref{chap:chapter3}, the main limitations of a GPD are the residual non-uniformities in the azimuthal response, also called spurious modulation, and rate-dependent gain variations. Both these problems can be attributed to the GEM, the GPD multiplication stage. The spurious modulation is caused by the manufacturing process of the GEM, where small shape variations between the holes induce a position-dependent systematic effect which takes a lot of time to finely characterize and correct.

On the other hand, rate-dependent gain variations are attributed to the large dielectric surface inside a GEM hole, which leads to the trapping of part of the charge produced during the avalanche. The consequence of the dielectric charging is a change in the configuration of the electric field inside the hole, which results in the decrease of the charge multiplication factor. If the detector is constantly exposed to radiation, charges accumulate with time and the gain keeps varying until the saturation is reached. The asymptotic saturation value is determined by the incoming energy flux, with higher fluxes leading to a larger gain change. This means that when the X-ray observatory switches between sources with different fluxes the detector is not stable until it reaches a new equilibrium state, requiring to apply complex time-dependent corrections in the meantime.

To overcome these limitations a new type of gas pixel detector, $\mu$GPD, which employs an innovative multiplication stage instead of the GEM, is under development. The idea behind $\mu$GPD is to use micro-gap-like structures \cite{sgro2026, angelini1993} built directly on top of an XPOL ASIC.


\section{Detector design}

\begin{figure}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{figures/chapter5/mugpd}
    \caption{Comparison between the current GPD configuration (left panel) and the new proposed configuration with a hexagonal two-dimensional multiplication structure (right). X-rays are absorbed by the active gas volume and emit a photoelectron, which drifts toward the electron multiplier structure. In both cases the track shape is preserved by the multiplication structure. Image taken from \cite{sgro2026}.}
    \label{fig:mugpd}

The $\mu$GPD represents a potential successor to the current GPD generation, relying on the same working principles as traditional photoelectric polarimeters. This means that the new detector has a gas active volume to absorb X-ray photons and produce a photoelectron, which is emitted in a direction that depends on the photon polarization. The photoelectron track produced by primary ionization is used to measure the emission angle, and for this purpose the charges are multiplied and subsequently collected by an ASIC placed below the multiplication stage.

The fundamental difference is in the multiplication stage, which in the $\mu$GPD final design consists of structures that are built on the upper-most layers of the wafer during the manufacturing of the ASIC. $\mu$GPD is therefore a monolithic detector with the multiplication stage integrated on the readout chip. A comparison between one of the proposed designs and the current GPD design is shown in Fig. \ref{fig:mugpd}. 

The design and the idea behind it come from micro-gap chambers, which are based on two metal layers separated by a thin isolation layer. The upper-most layer is segmented in one-dimensional or two-dimensional structures only a few $\mu$m large and acts as the anode, while the bottom layer is usually a plane and acts as the cathode. Applying a potential difference between the two electrodes generates a strong electric field in a small volume just above the anode. The primary ionization electrons drift toward the anode, where they are accelerated by the electric field and produce an avalanche. The avalanche electrons are collected by the anode, while the positive ions are collected by the bottom cathode. An example of the avalanche process is shown in Fig. \ref{fig:avalanche}. 

This represents a crucial difference with respect to current GPDs. While in current GPDs the readout signal is provided by the electrons collected on the XPOL ASIC, in this novel architecture the signal is carried by the positive ions collected at the cathode. Because the existing XPOL family of chips is exclusively designed to process negative charges, it is necessary to develop a completely new ASIC to read the positive ion signals.

This design is a good candidate to solve the issues of the current generation of GPD because the ASIC CMOS manufacturing processes allow to reach an accuracy on length scales of hundreds of nanometers, much smaller than the tolerances of the GEM manufacturing process. As a consequence, gain non-uniformities across the sensor are likely to be strongly reduced. Furthermore, the surface of exposed dielectric can be small and the amount of charge that can be accumulated is reduced, improving detector stability over time and gain variations.

The one-dimensional structures consist of multiple parallel strips that run along the pixels. There are different designs which are currently under study that vary in pitch and how they are aligned with respect to the pixels. In particular, one design has a pitch of 43.3 $\mu\text{m}$ (the vertical pitch of the pixels), with a strip aligned to the center of each pixel row. The other two designs have a larger pitch of 86.6 $\mu\text{m}$ (two times the vertical pitch of the pixels), where the strips are either aligned to the pixel centers or offset with respect to them. For the two-dimensional structure the anode has the same shape of the hexagonal pixels, thus each pixel is surrounded by the anode line along its perimeter.

\section{Prototype testing and performance}

The final goal of this research is the development of a fully monolithic detector where the multiplication stage is integrated directly on top of the readout ASIC during standard CMOS foundry fabrication, but before commissioning the final detector the feasibility of the design must be verified under realistic operating conditions through progressive steps:

\begin{enumerate}
\item fabricate one and two-dimensional structures on a plain dummy silicon wafer to verify that they can effectively act as a multiplication stage on a flat substrate;
\item fabricate the structures via photolithographic post-processing directly onto an existing XPOL ASIC to test layer deposition on an irregular, real chip surface;
\item verify that the ASIC survives the post-processing steps and operates properly before powering the multiplication structures;
\item verify that the multiplication structures function properly once integrated onto the chip;
\item verify that the ASIC continues to operate reliably while the multiplication stage is actively powered.
\end{enumerate}

To carry out these preliminary tests without relying on a full CMOS fabrication run, the current prototype structures are realized through a photolithographic post-processing technique. This approach allows depositing the insulating and anode layers directly on top of the substrate, closely replicating the operational conditions of the integrated architecture while drastically reducing development time and complexity.

The insulating layer consists of a high-dielectric-strength material (polyimide or SU-8) 2~$\mu$m thick and 13~$\mu$m wide, while the aluminum anode has a thickness of 2~$\mu$m and a width of 5~$\mu$m. It is worth noting that the current prototype leaves significantly more exposed dielectric surface than targeted in the final design. This is due to technical limitations in the photolithographic deposition process, which can be overcome by transitioning to standard CMOS fabrication.

Once the basic functionality of the structures is verified, the next crucial step is to characterize the gain variations and the achievable energy resolution. Various experimental tests have been performed under different operating conditions to assess the feasibility of these anode structures as the multiplication stage for the next generation of GPDs.

\subsection{Initial tests on a dummy silicon wafer}
The first test has been performed by depositing the structures on top of a high-resistivity silicon wafer which acts as the collection electrode, as shown in Fig. \ref{fig:mugpd_wafer}. This first demonstrator serves to verify the possibility of depositing these structures on top of a substrate similar to that of XPOL chips with photolithographic processes without deteriorating the material.

To test the structures the metallic layer of a custom PCB is placed in contact with the back of the wafer to provide negative high voltage, while the anode structures are kept at 0 V. The signal coming from the anode is read through a single channel spectroscopic amplifier which in turn is connected to a Multi-Channel Analyzer (MCA).

\begin{figure}[!b]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\linewidth]{figures/chapter5/mugpd_wafer}
    \includegraphics[width=0.4\linewidth]{figures/chapter5/si_wafer_test}
    \caption{Picture of the silicon wafer with the four anode structures built on top (left panel) and experimental setup for data acquisition with the wafer inside the gas chamber (right panel). Images taken from \cite{sgro2026}.}
    \label{fig:mugpd_wafer}
\end{figure}

To simulate the working conditions of a GPD, the setup is placed inside a dedicated gas chamber, flushed with a mixture of Ar/Isobutane 80/20. The absorption and drift region are defined by a 1 cm thick spacer with an electrode placed on top of the structures. 

X-ray photons are produced by a $^{55}$Fe source placed on top of the spacer, optionally with a collimator or an additional spacer to regulate the event rate. $^{55}$Fe is a radioactive isotope of iron which decays by electron capture into $^{55}$Mn emitting fluorescence radiation. In most cases the electron capture involves an electron in the $K$-shell, thus the resulting radiation belongs to the $K\alpha$ line at 5.9 keV ($\sim$90\% probability) and to the $K\beta$ line at 6.5 keV ($\sim$10\% probability).

For this initial test a particular focus is on the one-dimensional structures. The 43.3 $\mu$m and 86.6 $\mu$m pitch one-dimensional designs are tested for different anode-cathode voltages, and can withstand up to 380--400 V.

To measure how the charge multiplication factor of the two structures varies depending on the voltage difference the electronic chain made of the spectroscopic amplifier and the MCA needs to be calibrated with a charge injection circuit which has a known input capacitance. This calibration allows to convert ADC counts to the effective charge collected by the anode.

For these preliminary tests the observed spectra are fitted with a single Gaussian, even though the energy resolution is good enough to observe an asymmetry in the $K\alpha$ emission line due to the presence of the $K\beta$ line.

Knowing the nominal line energy and the average ionization energy $w$ of the gas, the detector charge multiplication factor is estimated from the mean of the fitted Gaussian, while the energy resolution is directly inferred from its FWHM.

The dependence of gain and energy resolution on the voltage difference of the micro-gap is shown in Fig. \ref{fig:wafer_performance}. For the gain the exponential behaviour described in Eq. \ref{eq:exp_gain} is clearly visible. For both structures, a gain of a few hundred is achievable with voltages below 400 V, on the same order of magnitude as the GEM gain in GPDs (which is about 200).

Furthermore, it can be seen that the energy resolution improves with increasing gain, and the best energy resolution is achieved by the 86.6 $\mu$m design, which is also the design that can reach a higher gain with lower voltages with respect to the 43.3 $\mu$m design.

These results prove that these structures deposited directly on top of an XPOL-like substrate can work as multiplication stage while operating at voltage differences up to 400 V. Furthermore, sufficiently high effective gain is achieved even at relatively low voltages, which is essential to reduce the possibility of destructive discharges and the overall stress on the detector, improving reliability and stability.

\subsection{Printing on XPOL ASIC}
After demonstrating that the structures can be successfully deposited on a substrate and operate as a multiplication stage, the next step is to fabricate them directly onto pre-existing chips via post-processing and verify that the ASIC survives the fabrication process.

Unlike a dummy silicon wafer, the ASIC surface is not perfectly planar. The individual pixels are isolated from one another by narrow, raised dielectric ridges surrounding each pixel boundary. This morphology poses a challenge for layer deposition, requiring the photolithographic process to be carefully tuned to avoid defects in both the insulating layer and the anode lines. In fact, a single discontinuity along a metal strip would compromise the continuity of the entire downstream electrode.

The best deposition results on the ASIC are achieved using SU-8 instead of polyimide, combined with a slight increase in the layer thickness. Indeed, this specific choice of material and thickness effectively mitigates the formation of defects over the dielectric ridges between the pixels, leading to a higher overall fabrication quality. An example of the structures fabricated with the standard polyimide dielectric on the chip surface is shown in Fig. \ref{fig:deposition_noise}, where the presence of these printing defects is clearly visible.

Once the successful deposition of the layers on the irregular chip surface is confirmed via SEM inspection, the next step is to evaluate whether the post-processing damaged the underlying ASIC electronics. For this purpose, preliminary electrical tests are carried out before placing the device in the gas chamber. By injecting test charges into the pixels, the readout is triggered and a large ROI is read. This procedure allows verifying the correct functionality of the pixels. It is worth noting that this diagnostic evaluation has been performed with the multiplication structures both with high voltage and at ground.

The measurements confirm that the chip survived the deposition process and is fully functional. Interestingly, mapping the pixel noise provides a sort of radiograph of the structures: pixels located under the structures show a slight change in the mean value. This effect also serves as a useful diagnostic tool to inspect the structures and identify potential fabrication defects, such as disconnected segments or discontinuities along the strips.

Fig. \ref{fig:deposition_noise} shows an example of a data acquisition performed without an X-ray source. In particular, it can be observed that the mean value read out by the pixels (after the online pedestal subtraction) is slightly modified by the presence of the structures. This suggests that the structures somehow affect the pixel behavior during the pedestal subtraction phase, meaning that the procedure would require recalibration. However, this is not a relevant aspect for what is intended to be demonstrated at this stage of development.

\subsection{Detector characterization with powered structures}
\begin{figure}[!b]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{figures/chapter5/box}
    \caption{The experimental setup before closing the gas chamber. The chip with the structures is placed below the yellow spacer. The collimator is then placed above the spacer, with an additional metal spacer that is used to reduce the observed event rate. The $^{55}$Fe source is the small cylinder on top of the metal spacer.}
    \label{fig:gembox}
\end{figure}
After proving that the structures can act as a multiplication stage on a flat substrate and that the ASIC survives the fabrication process, the final two steps are the following: verifying that the structures operate properly on the chip and characterizing their performance, and confirming that the ASIC survives the active multiplication process. Both steps require applying a high voltage difference between the anode structures and the chip pixels while the detector is irradiated by a radioactive source.

However, testing both aspects simultaneously is not possible with the current ASIC. In this micro-gap configuration, the chip acts as the cathodic collection plane, meaning it collects positive ions. Since the current XPOL chips are exclusively designed to collect electrons, they are intrinsically unable to read the avalanche signals produced by these structures. Therefore, the final validation must be decoupled into two different configurations.

Both tests are performed by placing the complete detector inside a sealed gas chamber, as shown in Fig. \ref{fig:gembox}. A 1 cm thick spacer with a top electrode defines the absorption and drift region, and the volume is continuously flushed with an Ar/Isobutane 80/20 gas mixture. X-ray photons are provided by a collimated $^{55}$Fe source placed on top of the chamber window.

The first test configuration is designed to evaluate the performance of the structures. To read the signal, the anode structures are kept at ground while a high negative voltage is applied to the chip. As for the silicon wafer test, the charge readout is performed by connecting the anodes to a single-channel spectroscopic amplifier and reading the output with a Multi-Channel Analyzer (MCA). Operating the chip at high voltage, a condition for which it is not designed, irreparably damages its electronics, but this configuration is strictly necessary to acquire the energy spectra and characterize the structures.

\begin{figure}[!b]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{figures/chapter5/pulse}
    \caption{Spectrum of injected calibration pulses with amplitude of 2, 4, 6 and 10 mV. Each line is independently fitted using a Gaussian model, and the means of the Gaussians are then used to calibrate the electronic chain. The calibration fit using $y = mx + q$ as fit model returns $m = 0.0212 \pm 0.0001$ fC/ADC count and $q = 0.192 \pm 0.003$ fC.}
    \label{fig:pulse}
\end{figure}

The second test configuration simulates the nominal operating conditions to verify if the chip can withstand the active multiplication process. The ASIC is kept at ground and the anode structures are powered with a high positive voltage. In this setup, it is impossible to read the avalanche signals on either the chip or the structures. However, this configuration allows the ASIC to operate normally. By injecting test charges into the pixels and studying the noise distribution, it is possible to monitor the front-end electronics and confirm that the chip survives without degradation while avalanches are generated right above it.

Starting with the first test configuration, the electronic chain must be calibrated to properly estimate the effective gain and energy resolution. This is achieved by injecting known amounts of charge using a circuit with a test capacitance. Square voltage pulses with different amplitudes are sent to the structures, and the resulting spectra are acquired with the MCA. Each peak is fitted with a Gaussian curve to get its mean position in ADC counts. Then, a linear fit of these mean ADC values as a function of the injected charge provides the calibration curve. An example of the calibration spectra is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:pulse}.

To study the structures performance multiple data acquisitions have been taken varying the anode-cathode voltage difference. Some acquired spectra are shown in Fig. \ref{fig:spectra}. From the spectra it is possible to observe the asymmetry of the main $K\alpha$ emission line at 5.9 keV due to the presence of the $K\beta$ line, as well as the argon escape peak at about 3.0 keV. All the structures show an energy resolution good enough to clearly separate the main peak from the escape peak.

\begin{figure}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\linewidth]{figures/chapter5/1d_43p3_spectrum}
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\linewidth]{figures/chapter5/1d_spectrum}
    \caption{Examples of $^{55}$Fe spectra acquired with the one-dimensional structures with 43.3 $\mu$m pitch (left panel) and with 86.6 $\mu$m (right panel). The energy resolution is estimated from the $K\alpha$ line. The peak visible at the left is the Ar escape peak.}
    \label{fig:spectra}

To estimate gain and energy resolution of the structures, the position and width of the $K\alpha$ line are employed. To take into account emission from both lines the spectra are fitted using a model with two Gaussians to deconvolve them. The model is

\begin{equation}
f(x; a, \sigma, b, r) = \frac{ar}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma} e^{\frac{(x - E_{\alpha})^2}{2\sigma^2}} + \frac{a(1-r)}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma'} e^{\frac{(x - E_{\beta}/b)^2}{2\sigma'^2}}, \quad \quad \sigma' = \sigma \sqrt{\frac{E_{\alpha}}{E_{\beta}}},
\label{eq:two_lines_fit}
\end{equation}
where $a$ is the amplitude, $b$ is the energy scale, $r$ is the probability of emitting a $K\alpha$ photon instead of a $K\beta$ and the standard deviation scales as $\sigma \sim 1/\sqrt{E}$.

The results obtained across multiple anode-cathode voltage differences are shown in Fig. \ref{fig:compare_results}. In agreement with expectation, the exponential dependence of the gain on the applied voltage is verified by fitting each dataset with the model described in Eq. \ref{eq:exp_gain}.

The structure with 86.6 $\mu$m pitch shows a higher gain compared to the 43.3 $\mu$m design at the same operating voltages. This difference can be explained by looking at the electric field configuration above the anode strips. When the pitch between the strips is larger, the electric field lines extend higher up into the gas volume. As a result, the primary electrons enter the high-field region earlier during their drift and experience a longer avalanche path, which leads to a larger charge multiplication. Furthermore, with a larger pitch, the field lines coming from the drift region focus more effectively onto the thin anode strip, concentrating the electric field and further enhancing the avalanche process.

In the same way that a smaller pitch decreases the gain, the two-dimensional hexagonal geometry pushes this effect even further. Since the anode runs all around the pixel perimeter, the amount of anode area is even larger, which further accentuates the effect described above. In fact, this is confirmed by the measurements, because to get enough gain to see a proper spectrum, the voltage difference has to be pushed above 400 V, and even with that, the effective gain is very low, around 40. It is worth noting that this is still a remarkable result, as these are the smallest micro-gap structures fabricated to date and the first two-dimensional ones to achieve enough gain to overcome the noise and observe a source spectrum. A spectrum acquired with this structure is shown in Fig. \ref{fig:spectrum_2d}.

As can be seen in Fig. \ref{fig:compare_results}, the energy resolution clearly depends on the applied voltage and gain. At low voltages, the resolution is quite poor because the signal is small and the electronic noise dominates. When the voltage increases, the gain grows and the signal becomes larger compared to the noise, improving the signal-to-noise ratio and making the energy resolution better. In particular, the 86.6 $\mu$m pitch structures show a significantly better energy resolution compared to the 43.3 $\mu$m ones, reaching an outstanding energy resolution of about 11.5\%, compared to 15\% achieved by the 43.3 $\mu$m. After a certain point, once the noise becomes negligible, the resolution reaches a plateau where it is limited only by the intrinsic statistical fluctuations of the gas ionization and avalanche. However, pushing the voltage even further causes the resolution to degrade again because of non-linearities and instabilities in the avalanche at very high gains.

Comparing these results with those obtained on the dummy silicon wafer (Fig. \ref{fig:wafer_performance}), it can be observed that the gain is significantly lower for both structures when operated on the ASIC. As mentioned earlier, this is due to the fact that printing the structures on the ASIC required an increased dielectric thickness. Consequently, the larger distance between the anode and the cathode weakens the electric field, which reduces the efficiency of the avalanche multiplication process. Regarding the energy resolution, however, the results obtained on the chip are much more representative. This is because the experimental setup used for the initial wafer acquisitions was not optimized and it introduced a higher amount of electronic noise, which became the dominant contribution degrading the resolution.

The same trend described for one-dimensional structures is visible also for the two-dimensional hexagonal geometry. Since these structures operate at a much lower gain, the signal stays closer to the noise level, leading to a worse energy resolution that only reaches about 23\%.

Moving on to the second test configuration, the test consisted of operating the ASIC at ground potential while biasing the multiplication structures with a positive high voltage. The complete detector was continuously powered inside the gas chamber and exposed to the radioactive source for over a day. After this prolonged operational period, the integrity of the underlying ASIC was verified by performing a new noise acquisition via charge injection into the pixels. The measured noise distribution showed no differences compared to the one acquired before applying the high voltage. This result successfully confirms that the chip electronics can reliably survive the continuous avalanche generation process without suffering any degradation.

It is worth noting that not all the tested structures and chips survived the high voltage differences during the two tests. Depending on the specific design, the breakdown voltage was found to be around 400 V, with the two-dimensional structures reaching slightly higher values up to 430 V. The breakdown process is driven by the very strong electric field localized in the narrow gaps between the electrodes. When the field exceeds the dielectric strength of the gas, it triggers destructive sparks that can cause irreversible short circuits and permanently damage both the anode structures and the underlying ASIC.

Another factor contributing to these discharges is the presence of large unpassivated conductor regions on the chip. In particular, the main bus line that connects all the strips of the same design (visible in Fig. \ref{fig:mugpd_sem}b) and the connections to the readout pads create localized field enhancements at their edges. A standard solution to solve this problem is the passivation of these non-active conductive regions with an insulating layer.

During additional tests, a preliminary passivation was made by depositing a layer of insulating glue over the main connections. This simple intervention effectively shielded the high-field regions at the edges, leading to a notable reduction in destructive spark events and allowing the structures to reach higher operating voltages. A picture showing the damage caused by a destructive discharge on the chip and the applied passivation layer is shown in Fig. \ref{fig:passivation}.


\subsection{Gain variations}

Regarding charging, which is the other effect causing the instability of current GPDs due to temporal gain variations, as described in Chap. \ref{chap:chapter3}, a study was carried out to evaluate the behavior of these new designs. The design under test is the 43.3 $\mu\text{m}$ pitch one-dimensional structure, and the data are acquired by applying an anode-cathode voltage of 380 V.

The detector was allowed to charge for about three hours, remaining powered on the entire time. After that, the voltage was turned off to start the discharging phase; during this period, the detector was powered on only for the thirty seconds needed to take each data acquisition.

The acquisitions were taken manually for about seven hours. Fig. \ref{fig:mugpd_charging} shows the variation of the gain, normalized to the first acquisition, as a function of time during both the charging and discharging phases. The measurement taken after twenty hours was performed the following morning.

\begin{figure}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{figures/chapter5/charging_fit}
    \caption{Charging and discharging processes for a detector with 43.3 $\mu$m pitch one-dimensional structures. The charging process is fitted with Eq. \ref{eq:stretched_exp}. The best-fit characteristic time is $\tau = 0.711 \pm 0.026$ h, with a stretching exponent $\gamma = 0.61\pm 0.02$.}
    \label{fig:mugpd_charging}
\end{figure}

The model used to fit the charging phase is a stretched exponential \cite{tomaiuolo2024}, described by

\begin{equation}
f(x; a, \tau, \gamma) = a \exp \left[ - \left( \frac{x - x_0}{\tau} \right)^{\gamma} \right].
\label{eq:stretched_exp}
\end{equation}
The charging process characteristic time is $\tau = 0.711 \pm 0.026$ h. 

The fit of the discharging phase was not performed due to the limited amount of data, which does not allow for a clear identification of when the plateau is reached and the detector recovers its initial gain value.

What can be observed is that the gain variation is quite large, reaching a decrease of more than 35\%. This is because these structures are only a prototype designed to test the feasibility of placing the amplification stage directly on top of the ASIC. In particular, the dielectric layer between the chip and the anode is much wider than the anode strip itself due to technical limitations of the employed deposition process. Consequently, a large dielectric surface is exposed and a significant amount of charge can accumulate on it, causing a strong variation in the electric field configuration and, as a result, in the gain.

\section{Conclusions}

At the current stage, the tests have demonstrated the feasibility of successfully printing and operating micro-gap structures directly on top of an XPOL ASIC without damaging the chip. This represents a breakthrough in the field of Micro-Pattern Gas Detectors, as the one-dimensional 43.3~$\mu$m layout represents the design with the smallest pitch ever operated.

Beyond proving the feasibility of the design, the characterization of the structures has shown outstanding results in terms of gain and, most notably, energy resolution, reaching 11.5\% (FWHM) for the 86.6~$\mu$m pitch structures. This demonstrates that this architecture represents a promising solution for the next generation of GPDs, which should likely ensure better uniformity across the detector, essential for reducing systematic effects in polarimetry measurements.

Further development of the post-processed structures will lead to improvements in gain and a reduction in charging effects. Specifically, this will be achieved by reducing the width of the dielectric layer to match that of the anode, minimizing the exposed insulating area. Furthermore, reducing the anode size itself will increase the gain by decreasing the electrode area, which consequently increases the electric field strength near the anode. These modifications will require further tests on the chip to verify that it can withstand these changes without incurring destructive discharges.

However, the ultimate goal of this project remains the development of a fully monolithic chip where the multiplication structures are fabricated directly in the foundry during the ASIC production, utilizing a CMOS process. There are no fundamental obstacles preventing this integration, and the tests conducted strongly support the feasibility of this approach. While this monolithic design will eliminate several post-processing issues, such as the challenges of layer deposition on an irregular surface, and will provide an even better fabrication accuracy down to a scale of a few hundred nanometers or less, several open questions remain for the final design. In particular, it must be investigated whether the thin oxide layer deposited between the cathode and the anode will be able to withstand the high voltage without experiencing electrical breakdown, and whether the rest of the chip can survive these operational conditions.

Another crucial step towards the final detector concerns the ASIC itself, which will need to be redesigned to operate with the opposite polarity compared to the current version. Collecting electrons instead of ions will allow the readout of individual pixels and the reconstruction of photoelectron tracks. Consequently, it will finally be possible to evaluate the polarimetric sensitivity of the $\mu$GPD, verifying whether the systematic effects typically introduced by the GEM are successfully mitigated as expected.
