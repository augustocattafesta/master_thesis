The Imaging X-ray Polarimetry Explorer (IXPE) \cite{weisskopf2021} is a mission launched in 2021 by NASA and ASI to study the polarization of bright X-ray sources, capable of performing imaging, spectroscopy and timing measurements.

%A demonstration of its capabilities is shown in Fig. \ref{fig:casa_combined_image}, which displays the polarimetric map of the supernova remnant Cassiopeia A, the first official science target observed by IXPE after its commissioning.

In five years of operations IXPE has observed and measured the polarization of about 100 sources, ranging from accreting stellar-mass and supermassive black holes in AGN to magnetars, supernova remnants, and pulsar wind nebulae. A map of the sources observed by IXPE during the first year of observations is shown in Fig. \ref{fig:ixpe_sources}.

Accreting systems usually exhibit a low polarization degree, typically in the 1--10\% range, which is produced by Compton scattering of photons off the accreting material. Other sources, such as pulsar wind nebulae and magnetars, have a much higher polarization level, often exceeding 20\%, which is driven by the extreme environments around these objects, where strong magnetic fields generate highly polarized synchrotron radiation.

Unlike imaging or spectroscpy observations, measuring the polarization of a source requires accumulating a very large number of photons to statistically infer its polarization degree from the azimuthal emission angle distribution of photoelectrons, described by Eq. \ref{eq:polarization_photo}. The instrumental polarimetric sensitivity is typically quantified by the Minimum Detectable Polarization at a 99\% confidence level ($\operatorname{MDP}_{99}$), which is calculated from the instrumental modulation factor $\mu$ as

\begin{equation}
\operatorname{MDP}_{99} = \frac{4.29}{\mu R} \sqrt{\frac{R + B}{T}},
\label{eq:mdp}
\end{equation} 
where $R$ is the source rate, $B$ is the background rate, and $T$ is the total observation time. This relation is essential to estimate the required exposure time based on the source flux. Crucially, as $\operatorname{MDP}_{99}$ scales inversely with the square root of time, detecting increasingly smaller polarization levels requires significantly longer observations. An example of IXPE sensitivity is shown in Fig. \ref{fig:mdp_flux}, where it can be seen that detecting a polarization degree of a few percent requires exposure times of the order of $1000$ ks.

    \label{fig:mdp_flux}
\end{figure}

IXPE observations have led to important results in different fields of high-energy astrophysics. For example, they enabled the mapping of magnetic field geometries and turbulence in supernova remnants, constrained the orientation of accretion disks around black holes, and performed phase-resolved polarimetry on accreting neutron stars, combining timing and polarization measurements to better understand the emission geometry. Overall, these findings have provided useful observational tests for theoretical models, demonstrating the crucial role of X-ray polarimetry in modern high-energy astrophysics.

IXPE represents a major breakthrough in X-ray astrophysics, as it is the first mission entirely dedicated to soft X-ray polarimetry. Its polarimetric sensitivity is more than an order of magnitude higher than Bragg crystal polarimeters that flown on board earlier missions. This major leap in performance was made possible by the innovations introduced by the Gas Pixel Detectors (GPDs) \cite{baldini2021}, the focal-plane detectors employed on board IXPE.

GPDs use photoelectric effect to infer the degree of polarization of a source, in particular they exploit the dependence of the emission angle of the photoelectron on the photon polarization. By collecting enough photons it is possible to measure the polarization of the source on a statistical basis, by studying the distribution of the emission angle measured for all the events.

Beyond its polarimetric capabilities, the observatory provides valuable spatial and spectral information. The GPD enables imaging spectroscopy with an energy resolution of about 17\% FWHM at 5.9 keV, providing the necessary energy discrimination to isolate different emission components in complex extended structures.

In the future, IXPE will continue its observational program by targeting fainter sources and monitoring variable sources over long time scales, providing new information on physics in extreme magnetic and gravitational fields.

\section{Optics and Mirror Module Assembly}

IXPE consists of three identical and independent X-ray telescopes. Using three independent telescopes provides redundancy and increases the total effective area while keeping the focal spot small to minimize background noise. 

Each telescope has its grazing incidence mirrors with Wolter-1 configuration and a focal length of 4 m. 
The mirrors, shown in Fig. \ref{fig:ixpe}, are made of 24 concentric and nested foils of nickel and cobalt separated by 2 mm. Each nested foil is 600 mm long, and it includes both the parabolic and hyperbolic segment of the Wolter-1 configuration. The entire layout and materials are optimized for high sensitivity in the energy range 2--8 keV.

    \includegraphics[width=0.49\linewidth]{figures/chapter3/MMAendview}
    \caption{Schematic of the IXPE Observatory (left panel). Photons enter through the three Mirror Module Assemblies and are collected by the Detector Units, where GPDs are located. The MMA (right panel) is made of concentric and nested foils of nickel. Images courtesy of NASA.}
    \label{fig:ixpe}
\end{figure}


To fit inside the launch vehicle fairing while maintaining this focal length, IXPE uses a deployable boom that was extended once in orbit, as shown in Fig. \ref{fig:ixpe}.

A detector unit (DU) is located on the focal plane of each telescope. Each detector unit houses a GPD to absorb and analyze X-ray photons, providing position, energy, timing and polarization information. Additionally, each DU is equipped with onboard calibration sources (such as $^{55}\text{Fe}$) that can be exposed to the detector during flight. These sources are used to perform periodic calibrations and continuously monitor detector performance, such as gain variations, energy resolution, and systematic effects over time.

The ground calibration of the optics was performed in a dedicated facility housing a 100-meter vacuum beam tube with an X-ray source at one end. At the opposite end, each MMA was mounted and characterized using an SDD and a CCD on the focal plane to measure the optics effective area and point spread function, respectively. These measurements showed that the effective area reaches a peak of about 200~cm$^2$ for each MMA, and while mounting a GPD the angular resolution is characterized by a half-power diameter (HPD, the diameter enclosing 50\% of the focused photons) of $\lesssim 30$ arcsec over a $12.9 \times 12.9\text{ arcmin}^2$ square field of view.

After calibrating the response of the MMAs, a DU was placed on the focal plane in place of the calibration detectors to characterize the response of the final assembly. This step was performed using polarized X-ray sources with a known degree of polarization, allowing for the end-to-end validation of the telescope polarimetric response prior to launch.

This ground calibration phase was essential, as IXPE is the first mission of its kind in soft X-ray polarimetry, meaning there are no pre-existing celestial sources with known polarization properties that can be used as standard calibration sources in orbit.

\section{Gas Pixel Detector}

The Gas Pixel Detector (GPD) represents an important innovation for Micro-Pattern Gas Detectors (MPGD) \cite{bellazzini2004, bellazzini2006}. In traditional gas detectors, reading out signals with high spatial resolution requires complex printed circuit board (PCB) connections to external electronics. However, this PCB approach becomes a big problem when using thousands of channels, because it increases electronic noise and cross-talk between nearby pixels.

To solve this problem, the GPD puts the readout electronics directly inside the anode of the detector. This is done using a custom CMOS Application-Specific Integrated Circuit (ASIC). The top metal layer of the chip forms a grid of small hexagonal pixels that act directly as the charge collecting anode. Beneath each pixel, the ASIC substrate and metal layers incorporate a complete electronic chain. This design allows gas detectors to have high spatial resolution and integration similar to solid-state detectors, while keeping the noise low.

The GPD works as an X-ray polarimeter thanks to the photoelectric effect, where the emission angle of the photoelectron depends on the polarization of the incoming photon, as described by Eq. \ref{eq:polarization_photo}. Photons pass through a 50 $\mu$m thick beryllium window and are absorbed in the gas volume. The primary photoelectron creates an ionization track. An electric field drifts these primary electrons toward the GEM, which amplifies the signal without losing the two-dimensional shape of the track. Finally, the electrons are collected by the pixelated ASIC anode.

By studying the two-diemensional charge distribution of these tracks, the GPD can reconstruct the photon absorption point for imaging purposes, and measure the emission angle to calculate the polarization of the source. A schematic representation of the GPD and a reconstructed 5.9 keV photoelectron track are shown in Fig. \ref{fig:gpd}.

\subsection{Gas cell}

The GPD's active gas volume consists of pure dimethyl-ether (DME, (CH$_3$)$_2$O). The choice of the gas is a complex trade-off between two competing requirements: photon absorption efficiency and polarimetric sensitivity. Heavy elements are usually preferred to increase the quantum efficiency, because the photoelectric cross section scales as $Z^5$ and allows more photons to be absorbed. However, polarimetry also requires a high modulation factor $\mu$, which depends on how well the direction of the photoelectron can be reconstructed. Light gas mixtures are much better for this purpose because they produce longer and straighter tracks.

The shape of the photoelectron track depends on two physical processes. First, the energy loss by ionization is described by the Bethe-Bloch formula (Eq.~\ref{eq:bethe_bloch}). Second, the elastic Coulomb scattering off atomic nuclei scales as $Z^2$. In low-$Z$ materials this scattering is weak, so the electron does not change direction too much near the interaction point. This keeps the track straight, maximizing the modulation factor and thus increasing the overall sensitivity to polarization.

In addition to quantum efficiency and scattering considerations, another fundamental constraint that drives the choice toward low-$Z$ elements is the $K$-edge energy. The full azimuthal modulation described by Eq. \ref{eq:polarization_photo} holds strictly for photoelectrons emitted from $s$-orbitals (the $K$-shell), while ejection from higher orbitals results in a significantly reduced angular asymmetry. Therefore, to ensure that photoelectric absorption across the entire 2--8~keV operational band occurs predominantly from the $K$-shell, the $K$-edge of the gas constituents must be below 2 keV.

As reported in Tab.~\ref{tab:k_edge}, oxygen ($K$-edge at $\approx 525$~eV) is the heaviest element that satisfies this condition, which prevents the use of heavier noble gases traditionally employed in proportional counters, such as argon ($K$-edge at $3.2$~keV) or xenon.

DME is therefore a good compromise: its heaviest element is oxygen, and its low average $Z$ optimizes the track length and straightness needed for high polarimetric sensitivity. Furthermore, its quenching properties help prevent electrical discharges inside the detector. Finally, DME has a very low transverse diffusion coefficient (about $68~\mu\text{m cm}^{-1/2}$ at 2~kV/cm), which limits the blurring of the track while electrons drift toward the GEM and preserves the track shape needed to measure the photoelectron emission direction.

\subsection{Gas Electron Multiplier}
The GEM is the amplification stage of the GPD, where the primary ionization electrons arrive under the influence of the drift electric field inside the active volume. The strong electric field inside the holes, generated by the voltage difference between the two sides of the GEM, causes charges to produce an avalanche.

The GEM employed inside the GPD is made of liquid crystal polymers (LCP) with a 50 $\mu$m thickness, while the coating is made of copper and each side is 5 $\mu$m thick, for a total thickness of 60 $\mu$m. The holes have a diameter of 30 $\mu$m and follow hexagonal grid pattern to match the ASIC pixel pattern, with 50 $\mu$m pitch on the horizontal direction and 43.3 $\mu$m on the vertical one. The voltage difference between the two opposite sides is of 470 V, ensuring an effective gain of about 200.

The GPD's GEM is shown in Fig. \ref{fig:gem}, and it is possible to observe the presence of eight equally spaced vertical lines. These lines are a result of the manufacturing process, which includes a step where the holes are drilled with a laser. The laser beam has a width smaller than the active region, and it needs multiple sweeps to complete the entire area.

These non-uniformities cause the appearance of systematic effects that affect the GPD performance, and will be discussed in Chap. \ref{subsec:sys_eff}.

\subsection{XPOL-I}
The GPD's ASIC, named XPOL-I, the first generation of the XPOL family, acts as the readout anode and is responsible for the collection of charges produced by the GEM during the multiplication stage.

The chip is organized as a matrix of \num{105600} hexagonal pixels, with 300 columns and 352 rows, with a horizontal pitch of 50.00 $\mu$m and a vertical pitch of 43.30 $\mu$m, as shown in Fig. \ref{fig:gpd_layout}. This is the layout that was replicated on the GEM in order to align each hole to the correspondent pixel.

\begin{figure}[!b]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\linewidth]{figures/chapter3/gpd_layout}
    \caption{Geometrical layout of the readout XPOL-I ASIC. The pixels have a hexagonal shape, with horizontal pitch of 50.00 $\mu$m and a vertical pitch of 43.30 $\mu$m. The total active area of the chip is $15 \times 15$ mm$^2$. The inset shows the morphology of a trigger mini-cluster. Image taken from \cite{baldini2021}.}
    \label{fig:gpd_layout}
\end{figure}

A pixel consists of a hexagonal metal electrode, which is connected to its own charge-sensitive amplifier and shaping circuit. The ASIC is self-triggering and has an on-chip signal processing to localize the position of an event. After an event triggers the chip, the maximum of the shaped pulse is stored inside each pixel for readout.

The data acquisition (DAQ) is performed through a dedicated board with an Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC) and a Field Programmable Gate Array (FPGA). The ADC is responsible for the serial readout, with a resolution of 14 bits and a full dynamic range of 2.4 V. Considering the GEM gain, this corresponds to a  resolution of 2.3 electrons/ADC count. The FPGA controls various aspects of the detector, including the ASIC configuration, readout and data formatting.

The trigger logic is based on the so-called mini-clusters, which are sets of four adjacent pixels that are logically OR-ed together. An example of mini-cluster is shown in the inset of Fig. \ref{fig:gpd_layout}. When the OR-ed shaped signal exceeds the trigger threshold, the event is triggered. The trigger region consists of the smallest rectangle that contains all triggered mini-clusters.

Given that the shape of a photoelectron track is elongated and the portion that triggers the event is usually limited to its end, which corresponds to the Bragg peak, the chip automatically extends the readout region with a padding of 4 or 5 additional mini-clusters along each side of the $x$ and $y$ coordinates, respectively. This extended region, usually called Region Of Interest (ROI), allows to be certain of including the entire track in the ROI.

The ASIC calculates the coordinates of the upper-left and lower-right points of the ROI and then the DAQ board serially reads all the pixels inside it, which are typically around 500--800 pixels. The ability of the ASIC to limit the readout to a small region allows the total readout time to be reduced of more than two orders of magnitude with respect to a full chip readout.

A peculiar feature of XPOL-I is that the ROI is sequentially read two times in close succession. The first readout is used to read the pixel values, while the second one is used to perform online pedestal subtraction. Furthermore, the resulting data are zero-suppressed, allowing for a compression of the event size.

The choice of performing an event-by-event online pedestal subtraction was made to cancel all time-dependent baseline drifts and transient systematic effects. These include small charge leakage effects in the hold capacitors during the serial readout delay, as well as transients induced by the ASIC digital activity. These systematic shifts cannot be corrected using a static, full-chip offline calibration map because the position, size, and shape of the ROI change for every event.

Since the time at which a pixel is sampled relative to the trigger depends on its position within the specific ROI, the same pixel experiences different readout delays and digital transients depending on whether it is located at the beginning or at the end of the readout sequence. By reading the exact same ROI twice in close succession, these location and time-dependent baseline variations are effectively subtracted and canceled out.

The event readout capture timing of XPOL-I is shown in Fig. \ref{fig:xpol_timing}. In the basic self-triggering XPOL readout mode the \textit{TrigOut} signal is generated when at least a minicluster exceeds the trigger threshold and prevents additional triggers until it is externally reset. \textit{T$_{dh}$} is the time delay between the arrival of the event and the threshold crossing, and it is smaller than the shaper time \textit{T$_{pk}$}.

After the \textit{TrigOut} signal is asserted, the \textit{Track} signal is asserted and after a period of peak detection of 10 $\mu$s the \textit{Hold} signal freezes the analog output data. At the same time the \textit{DataReady LVDS} output is asserted, and the serial readout sequence can start.

Once the ROI has been defined, the serial readout is driven by the readout clock and the output of each pixel is routed to the differential output buffer connected to the external ADC. One of the most important parameter is \textit{Tasu}, which is the time necessary to the output buffer to reach the target value for the ADC to sample. This time is of the order of 200 ns, limiting the maximum clock frequency for serial readout to 5 MHz. The serial readout timing is shown in Fig. \ref{fig:xpol_timing}

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{figures/chapter3/xpol_timing0}
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{figures/chapter3/xpol_timing1}
    \caption{XPOL event capture timing (top panel) and serial readout timing (bottom panel)}
    \label{fig:xpol_timing}

At the end of the readout sequence, the reset of the analog signal of the pixels and all the trigger variables, such as the region of interest coordinates, is performed by a signal labeled \textit{AnaResetbar}, which also inibits the trigger for 150 $\mu$s.

The most relevant steps that contribute to the deadtime budget are the signal serial readout ($\sim$ 120 $\mu$s), the pedestal delay (500 $\mu$s) which is necessary for the signal in the sample and hold to decay before starting the pedestal readout (which takes $\sim$ 120 $\mu$s) and finally the subtraction ($\sim$ 120 $\mu$s). Finally there is the analog reset delay (150 $\mu$s), for a total of more than 1 ms for the readout of an event.

Some of these depend on the ROI size, while the delays are constant, thus the deadtime can be separeted into two independent contributes. The relatively long readout time is one of the limitations of XPOL-I. Reducing it is essential for the next generation of X-ray polarimetry observatories, which with a larger mirror effective area than IXPE will observe much higher rates even from moderately bright sources and the current GPDs would not be able to take advantage of it.

\subsection{Systematic effects}
\label{subsec:sys_eff}

The GEM manufacturing process directly affects the GPD performance by introducing a spatially dependent systematic effect known as spurious modulation, as shown in Fig. \ref{fig:spurious_mod}. The primary origin of this feature lies in the laser drilling sweeps used during GEM production because microscopic spatial non-uniformities in the hole geometry or pitch along the boundaries of adjacent sweeps create a regular pattern of artificial modulation across the active area.

\begin{figure}[!b]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\linewidth]{figures/chapter3/spurious_mod}
    \caption{Modulation amplitude map obtained from an un-polarize X-ray source at 2.7 keV. The dashed gray grid correspond to the coordinates of the laser sweep overlaps (visible in Fig. \ref{fig:gem}) during the GEM production, and it perfectly overlaps with the regions with high-spurious modulation. Image taken from \cite{baldini2021}.}
    \label{fig:spurious_mod}
\end{figure}

In principle, this systematic effect could be calibrated out by acquiring high-statistics response maps at multiple energies. However, mapping the detector at the spatial scale of the point spread function with the required statistical precision would require a prohibitively long calibration time for each flight unit.

To overcome this limitation without compromising spatial resolution, the adopted solution relies on spacecraft dithering during sky observations. By slowly moving the pointing direction along the line of sight, the image of a celestial source is broadened over a larger area of the detector plane in detector coordinates. This spatial averaging smooths out the localized non-uniformities caused by the GEM holes pattern, effectively mitigating the spurious modulation while preserving the intrinsic angular resolution of the telescope.

As a result, it is no longer necessary to calibrate the spurious modulation at the fine spatial scale of the PSF. Instead, it is sufficient to apply the correction on spatial scales comparable to the region covered by the dithering pattern, which is of the order of some mm$^2$, reducing the calibration time required.

Another important effect that depends on the GEM is rate-dependent gain variations. When the detector is operating and is irradiated by a source, part of the charge that is produced by the avalanche inside the GEM holes is temporarily deposited onto the dielectric substrate. This charging phenomenon has the effect of modifying the electric field configuration, with a consequent decrease of the gain.

When the detector is constantly illuminated by a fixed rate source, the gain does not decrease indefinitely but reaches a saturation level after which it remains approximately constant. The asymptotic gain value and time scale of the charging process depend on the input energy flux.

This charging effect is reversible because the charge trapping on the dielectric substrate is not permanent. This phenomenon, which consists of a discharging of the dielectric, is a competing process of charging and is continuously at play, but on much longer time scales. When the incoming flux is sufficiently low, discharging dominates over charging and the gain increases.

In general charging and discharging can be described as rate-dependent, and an example of these processes at play for GPD is shown in Fig. \ref{fig:rate_gain}.

A possible phenomenological model to describe gain variations as a function of time and input energy flux considers the two competing processes of charging and discharging, which act simultaneously but on different time scales \cite{baldini2021}:

\begin{equation}
\frac{dg(t)}{dt} = - \frac{F(t)}{k_c}(\delta_{\textrm{max}} + g(t)) + \frac{g(t)}{\tau_d},
\label{eq:diff_gain}
\end{equation}
where $g(t)$ is the relative gain variation, $F(t)$ is the input energy flux, $k_c$ and $\tau_d$ are two constants describing the characteristic time scales of the two processes, and $\delta_{\textrm{max}}$ is the maximum relative gain decrease observed for the detector.

If the input energy flux $F$ is constant, Eq. \ref{eq:diff_gain} can be solved analytically, yielding an effective time constant for the overall process given by

\begin{equation}
\tau_{\textrm{eff}} (F) = \left( \frac{1}{\tau_c} + \frac{1}{\tau_d} \right)^{-1},
\label{eq:tau}
\end{equation}
where $\tau_c = k_c / F$ represents the charging time scale. Under these conditions, the asymptotic relative gain variation is expressed as

\begin{equation}
\delta(F) = - \frac{\delta_{\textrm{max}}}{1 + \frac{\tau_c}{\tau_d}}.
\label{eq:delta}
\end{equation}

Typical values for the charging parameters are $\delta_{\textrm{max}} \approx 0.1$, $k_c \approx 10^5$ keV mm$^{-2}$, and $\tau_d \approx 10^5$ s. Fig.~\ref{fig:rate_gain} shows how the asymptotic gain variation and the effective time constant vary as a function of the incoming energy flux.

Reducing the systematic effects that have been observed with this type of GEM is the goal for the next generation of GPD, which is currently under study. One of the alternative currently being developed will be presented in Chap. \ref{chap:chapter5}.

    $\vcenter{\hbox{\includegraphics[width=0.54\linewidth]{figures/chapter3/charging}}}$
    \caption{Charging and discharging effects as a function of time for GPD (left panel). The different colors data points represent gain measurements taken with different input energy fluxes. First the charging is dominant with a high-flux source which causes a gain decrease of nearly 10\%. When using a lower flux source the gain gradually recovers. The asymptotic gain variation and the time required to reach it (right panel) can be calculated from Eqs. \ref{eq:tau} and \ref{eq:delta}. Images taken from \cite{baldini2021}.}
    \label{fig:rate_gain}

Regarding the systematic effects introduced by the ASIC, it has been observed that its digital activity during the readout induces a tiny shift of the pixel baseline which is manifested as vertical structures in the ROI with an even-odd pattern and an amplitude of about 10 electrons. The reason behind this effect could be related to intrinsic asymmetries in the ASIC layout, such as the horizontal direction of the serial readout or how the bias voltage is distributed across the chip. This effect is partially canceled by the online pedestal subtraction, but the residual effect contributes to the spurious modulation.

\section{XPOL-III}
\label{sec:xpol3}
XPOL-III \cite{minuti2023} is the new generation of ASIC designed for high-throughput X-ray polarimetry. As mentioned above, GPDs present a few aspects that need to be improved to meet the requirements of the next polarimetry observatories, such as the enhanced X-ray Timing and Polarimetry (e-XTP) mission.

In this regard, the new ASIC needs to be capable of analyzing higher rates than the current XPOL-I on board of the IXPE mission. This has been achieved with XPOL-III by improving many different aspects.

There are two major aspects that have been improved in order to speed up the readout. The first is reducing the average size of the ROI and the second is modifying the readout electronics design to increase the clock frequency and reduce unnecessary delays. 

The chip has a layout similar to XPOL-I, with hexagonal pixels over a grid of 304 columns and 352 rows. The pixels act as anodes and are responsible for charge collection. The trigger logic is the same as that of XPOL-I, with mini-clusters representing the smallest building block of the event trigger. The smallest rectangle that contains all the triggered mini-clusters takes the name of Region Of Trigger (ROT).

The first difference with respect to XPOL-I is that the chip calculates and stores the upper-left and lower-right coordinates of the ROT, instead of the ROI. The second difference is that XPOL-III allows to choose a custom padding on each side of the ROT for each event, instead of a fixed one for all events as in the previous generation. By using the coordinates of the ROT and the padding information it is possible to define the ROI.


\begin{figure}[htpb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.54\linewidth]{figures/chapter3/track}
    \includegraphics[width=0.45\linewidth]{figures/chapter3/track_xpol3}
    \caption{Comparison between the real photoelectron tracks produced by a 5.9 keV source read with XPOL-I (left panel) and XPOL-III (right panel). It is evident that the size of the ROI is noticeably reduced with the new ASIC generation, going from ~1000 pixels to ~300 pixels. Images taken from \cite{baldini2021, minuti2023}.}
    \label{fig:xpol_tracks}
\end{figure}

Another important change is the transition from a DC-coupled trigger to an AC-coupled trigger. Variations of the DC offsets across different mini-cluster amplifiers made it necessary to set a high trigger threshold for XPOL-I. With the AC coupling this issue is overcome, allowing the threshold to be lowered. The consequence is that the chip is more sensitive to the low density ionization portion of the photoelectron track, the ROT includes almost the entire track and the necessary padding is reduced. 

As a result of these changes, the average ROI size is about three times smaller than XPOL-I, allowing to reduce the amount of time required for the readout. A comparison between the XPOL-I and XPOL-III ROIs is shown in Fig. \ref{fig:xpol_tracks}. 

The ROI average size reduction, combined with a deep renovation of the readout electronics to increase of up to a factor two the maximum serial readout clock with respect to XPOL-I and to improve the overall readout logic, enables to reduce the average readout time from about 1 ms to about 150 $\mu$s.
