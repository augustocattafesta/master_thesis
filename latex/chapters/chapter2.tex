\chapter{Detectors for X-ray Astronomy}
\label{chap:chapter2}

Since its birth in the 1960s \cite{giacconi2003}, X-ray astronomy has represented a powerful tool to study high-energy phenomena across the universe. The possibility to observe photons with energies above a few hundred eV produced by galactic and extragalactic sources, such as neutron stars, binary systems, supernova remnants or active galactic nuclei (AGN), is made possible by the small probability of interaction with the low-density interstellar medium (ISM) and intergalactic medium (IGM).

The main problem of X-ray astronomy arises when photons reach Earth's atmosphere, which is much denser than ISM and IGM, thus they are easily absorbed there and can not reach the surface. For comparison, atmosphere's outer layers have a density of about $10^{17}$ atoms cm$^{-3}$, while for ISM the average on the galactic plane is 1 atom cm$^{-3}$ and for IGM is $10^{-6}$--$10^{-7}$ atoms cm$^{-3}$ \cite{longair2011}, which correspond to mean free paths of about 10 m, 3 kpc and 3 Gpc for a 1 keV photon, respectively. The dependence of the mean free path on the medium density for hydrogen and nitrogen is shown in Fig. \ref{fig:mean_free_path}.

    \caption{Dependence of the mean free path of a 1 keV photon on the density of atoms, considering mediums composed by hydrogen or nitrogen.}
    \label{fig:mean_free_path}
\end{figure}

The presence of the atmosphere makes it necessary to build space telescopes on board of satellites and send them into orbit around the Earth or the Sun. This is the reason why X-ray astronomy was born only after the progress of rocket science, when it became possible to launch the first detectors on sub-orbital flights, with which the first extrasolar sources were discovered, and then on satellites, which allowed to improve observations thanks to the increased available observation time and to fully dedicates missions. Fig. \ref{fig:transparency} shows how the altitude at which atmosphere becomes transparent varies with photon energy.

This opportunity opened a new perspective on the universe, which led to the discovery of new types of sources and to a better understanding of the physical processes that take place in astrophysical sources during high-energy events. An example is Scorpius X-1, the first detected extrasolar X-ray source and the most luminous persistent source of the X-ray sky. It has an X-ray luminosity about $10^3$ times larger than its optical counterpart, indicating that the emission processes are very different from those of the Sun, which have an X-ray luminosity about $10^{-6}$ times smaller than the visible light. Indeed, Scorpius X-1 is a low mass X-ray binary system (LMXB) where a compact object, a neutron star in this case, accretes material from its companion and the emission is produced by the conversion of gravitational energy into thermal energy.

Many other X-ray sources of various nature were discovered in the following years, such as AGN, which are extragalactic sources where a supermassive black hole with a mass larger than $10^6$ M$_{\odot}$ accretes matter from the surrounding environment releasing enormous amounts of energy. All of these high-energy sources are united by the presence of extreme conditions, with gas heated to very high temperatures up to $10^{10}$ K or with high energy particles that interact with matter or magnetic fields producing high energy photons in the X and $\gamma$ bands of the electromagnetic spectrum. 

Due to the extremely faint fluxes arriving from astrophysical sources, achieving high sensitivity is a primary challenge in X-ray detection. For reference, during active phases the Sun can reach a flux of $10^6$ ph cm$^{-2}$ s$^{-1}$ in the range 2--10 keV while Scorpius X-1 has a flux of about 30 ph cm$^{-2}$ s$^{-1}$ and 3C 273, the most luminous AGN, about 0.01 ph cm$^{-2}$ s$^{-1}$. The necessity to improve sensitivity and other detection capabilities, in order to discover fainter sources and characterize them in greater detail, has led the research and development of X-ray detectors, which have evolved substantially over the years and have employed very different technologies.

\section{Observables}

The detection of light in any region of the electromagnetic spectrum can be summarized by the study of four fundamental observables: energy, arrival direction, arrival time and polarization. An ideal detector should be capable of measuring all of these properties at the same time, but in practice it is difficult to do it or to achieve a good resolution for all of them. Each of these observables is important to study different aspects of an astrophysical source. 

Measuring photon energy in X-ray astrophysics is important because the spectrum contains crucial information about the state of matter and the dominant emission processes. The most common emission processes in high energy sources can be divided into thermal and non-thermal processes. The former are temperature dependent and an example is black-body emission, which takes place on the hot and optically thick surface of neutron stars. Non-thermal emission can be produced by charged ultrarelativistic particles through different mechanisms, such as synchrotron radiation when interacting with a magnetic field or inverse Compton effect when the scattering with a low energy photon takes place. Another example of non-thermal emission is that produced by atomic transitions, and being able to resolve and separate different lines in the spectrum gives the possibility to probe the source chemical composition. An example of X-ray spectrum for the supernova remnant Cas A is shown in Fig. \ref{fig:cas_a}. 

High-resolution imaging is important to resolve and identify X-ray sources, allowing to match their counterparts in other regions of the electromagnetic spectrum. Furthermore, it is possible to study the morphology of extended sources, which can give crucial information on how high energy events originate, how they evolve and probe their innermost structures, which are not observable at lower energies due to the higher optical depth. An example that shows the importance of high resolution imaging is the study of supernova remnants, that allows to understand the explosion dynamics and the presence of asymmetry during the collapse. When combined with high-resolution spectroscopy, it becomes possible to separate shells of ejecta with different chemical composition, giving insights into final evolutionary stages of a massive star's life. A high resolution X-ray image of Cas A is shown in Fig. \ref{fig:cas_a}.

Studying temporal variations in the arrival time of photons allows to understand and monitor time dependent phenomena, which can occur on timescales ranging from years to milliseconds. In particular observing phenomena on short timescales can give important information about compact objects, such as pulsars and black holes in binary systems. By tracking variations of rotational period, it is possible to understand how accreting mechanisms work. High resolution timing enables the validation of fundamental theories such as general relativity and even place constraints on equations of state of neutron stars. 

Polarization measurements reveal details about the source geometry and its surrounding environment. In particular, these measurements enable to probe how magnetic fields are distributed, how they interact with matter and the impact on emission processes \cite{xray_polarimetry}. Another important aspect is the possibility to measure how X-rays interact with matter around the source, because scattering processes can polarize light. An interesting example is the study of magnetic fields around supernova remnants, which are responsible for galactic cosmic rays acceleration, and pulsars, where magnetic fields are at the basis of the emission of relativistic jets.


\section{A brief history of X-ray Astrophysics}
\label{section:history}

The first orbiting satellites to employ instrumentation to observe X-rays were part of the Orbital Solar Observatory (OSO) program, but they were mainly focused on the study of the Sun X-ray emission. Despite their main mission goals, they also performed some observations of galactic and extragalactic sources that allowed to confirm discoveries made by earlier observations with sub-orbital flights. However, without dedicated instrumentation it was not possible to carry on systematic studies of fainter sources. 

In this context, a major advancement was achieved with UHURU, the first orbital mission fully dedicated to X-ray observations of extrasolar sources, launched in 1970. Before UHURU, each sub-orbital flight was able to observe the sky for about five minutes, and during the entire 1960s only about an hour of observations was cumulatively gathered. With the launch of UHURU, observations were carried continuosly for over two years, collecting an amount of data many orders of magnitude larger than all the previous decade. This satellite performed an all-sky survey, scanning a different region of the sky each day. The results allowed to discover more than 300 new sources \cite{4ucatalog}, localized with a precision of 1 arc min, enabling the identification of counterparts in other regions of the electromagnetic spectrum, such as visible and radio.

The direct successors to UHURU were the missions of the High Energy Astronomy Observatories (HEAO) program for X-ray and $\gamma$-ray astrophysics. One of the most important among them is HEAO-2, known as Einstein Observatory, launched in 1978. Einstein Observatory had, strictly speaking, the first true X-ray telescope, meaning that for the first time mirrors were used to focus X-rays onto a detector located in the focal plane, marking the birth of imaging X-ray astronomy and paving the way to all the future detectors of this type.

Until that moment, an X-ray observatory usually had a set of collimators to mechanically delimit the observed sky region. With different collimator geometries and with dedicated reconstruction algorithms it was possible to achieve a resolution of at most a few arc sec (about 7--10 arc sec for HEAO-1 \cite{heao1a3}), but it was not possible to produce images of extended sources. Moreover, a better spatial resolution in a collimator usually implies a decrease of the effective area, with the consequent drop of the signal-to-noise ratio.

With Einstein Observatory these problems were solved using focusing optics. However, while for visible light a mirror surface reflects almost all the incoming photons, high-energy photons have a very high probability of penetrating the surface and thus not being reflected. The solution consists of using grazing-incidence optics, relying on total reflection of light to have an increase of reflectivity. Indeed, this law holds for X-rays too, with the difference that the total reflection angle is usually smaller than 1 degree and there is a strong dependence on the photon energy, making this method feasible only for low energy X-rays up to 10--20 keV.

A possible optics geometry is the Wolter, named after its designer Hans Wolter, that consists of two mirrors used to focalize light with low incidence angles, usually one with parabolic section and one with hyperbolic or ellipsoid section. This layout allows to have a field of view large enough to be used in astrophysics and it is one of the reasons that makes it the most common layout for grazing-incidence telescopes. The main issue of grazing-incidence telescopes is their very small reflective geometrical area due to the small inclination of the mirrors with respect to the optical axis. The solution to this problem is to use multiple nested concentrical mirrors to increase the area, altough it introduces a series of technical and structural problematics that have been overcome in the years thanks to technological improvements.

Einstein Observatory employed four nested mirrors with a diameter varying between 33 and 56 cm, a focal lenght of 3.4 m, a geometrical area of about 400 cm$^2$ and the capability of alternating four different detectors on the focal plane. For high resolution imaging, the effective area dropped down to 20 cm$^2$ at 0.25 keV and 5 cm$^2$ at 2 keV, with a spatial resolution of 2 arc sec \cite{heao2}. Other imaging and spectroscopy instruments on board were able to achieve an effective area between 100--200 cm$^2$. Results were remarkable and the first high-resolution images of X-ray sources were produced, allowing to study their morphology in a completely new electromagnetic band, even though the effective area was smaller than HEAO-1. This was possible because, contrary to collimator observatories, the entire effective area was focused on a small region of the sky, reducing background signal and consequently increasing the signal-to-noise ratio and the instrumental sensitivity. Fig. \ref{fig:tycho} shows a high-resolution image of the Tycho supernova remnant taken with Einstein Observatory.

The successive step in the creation of a new and more populated all-sky catalogue was lead by ROSAT, launched in 1990. ROSAT had a design very similar to Einstein Observatory, but the optics layout consisted in larger and more efficient mirrors and a different focal ratio, increasing both effective area and field of view, in order to rapidly scan the entire sky. Indeed, ROSAT had a field of view twice as large as Einstein and it also had a more uniform sensitivity across the field of view. In just 6 months, ROSAT was able to observe almost \num{150000} sources \cite{rosat}.

During its nine years of operations, ROSAT also studied in detail many different sources, including emission from galactic stars and compact objects and spectral and morphological studies of supernova remnants. For the first time, it was also able to resolve the diffuse extragalactic emission into tens of thousands of AGN, giving a fundamental contribution to the development of the AGN unified model and to emission variability studies. The limit of ROSAT was its narrow energy range, which was partially attributable to the mirrors geometry, that had a relatively big angle of inclination with respect to the focal axis to achieve a large field of view sacrifying sensitivity to higher energies, but was also due to the employed detectors. Indeed, the main types of imaging detectors used until ROSAT were Position-Sensitive Proportional Counters (PSPC) and Micro-Channel Plates (MCP).

PSPC are a class of gas detectors and they loose sensitivity at high energies because the gas becomes optically thin, with a consequent reduction of the quantum efficiency. For similar reasons, MCPs are unefficent at high energies, because X-rays easily penetrate the internal coating and glass. Furthermore, even though MCPs were the only way to achieve very good spatial resolutions, they are not capable to measure energy, requiring to alternate different instruments in the focal plane to study morphology and spectrum of a source.

A new mission was launched in 1993 to overcome these limitations, the Advanced Satellite for Cosmology and Astrophyisics (ASCA) mission, with two revolutionary changes. The first was in the optics design, that for the first time in a space telescope were realized using a large number of nested concentric metal foils coated with a high-Z metal. ASCA had four telescopes, each with 120 nested gold-coated foils positioned with a very small angle with respect to the optical axis, increasing both the maximum observable energy up to 12 keV and the effective area, which reached 325 cm$^2$ at 1 keV for each telescope (without considering detector efficiency). The second innovation consisted in the use of CCDs as detectors in the focal plane, the first time for an X-ray telescope. With the use of CCDs in photon counting mode it became possible to perform high resolution imaging and spectroscopy of the source at the same time, even at high energies.

The first generation of X-ray CCDs suffered from various problems, such as low quantum efficiency both at low and high energies, with the latter caused by a small depletion region, and electronical issues were responsible for spectral resolution deterioration. These problems were solved by the following generation of X-ray observatories, Chandra and XMM-Newton, both launched in 1999 and currently operational.

These two missions pushed to the limit the technological advancement of CCDs. XMM-Newton is the first X-ray observatory to employ a high thickness and fully depleted CCD \cite{xmm_pn}, ensuring high quantum efficiency in the entire energy range of operation, which reaches 15 keV. On the other hand, Chandra's CCDs were optimized for low energy X-ray photons and spatial resolution, improving the readout electronics to decrease noise and also reducing the pixel size, in order to take full advantage of the 10 meters long focal length and achieve a spatial resolution of $0.5$ arc sec \cite{chandra}.

Regarding polarimetry, the development of detection technologies has been slower than other fields due to difficulties in realizing efficient detectors. The first successful X-ray polarimetry mission took place in 1971 and was carried out with sub-orbital rockets using a crystal Bragg polarimeter, resulting in the first measure of the polarization level of the Crab Nebula.

This type of detector consists of a crystal of a specific material, which defines a narrow energy sensitivity band, mounted at an angle of 45 degrees with respect to the incident flux. If a photon has the electric field vector parallel to the crystal surface it is reflected, otherwise it penetrates the crystal and is absorbed. By counting the photons that are reflected as a function of the azimuthal angle while the detector is spinning around the axis that points towards the source, it is possible to measure the level of polarization.

For many years this method represented the only feasible way to measure X-ray polarization, but there were many physical and technical problems associated to it. First of all, the sensitive energy range is very small, even less than 1 eV, thus the event rate is small and the consequence is that even for bright sources the observational time required to detect polarization at a high confidence level is very long. The second problem is technical, because it is difficult to make the detector spin pointing exactly towards the source, with the possibility of introducing systematic errors that could false the polarization.

For these reasons a mission completely dedicated to X-ray polarimetry was never realized until a new technique to measure polarization was developed. This new method consists of using the dependence on the polarization of the emission direction of photoelectrons emitted by photoelectric effect. By tracking photoelectrons it is possible to infer the emission angle.

This is the idea behind Imaging X-ray Polarimetry Explorer (IXPE), launched in 2021, where Gas Pixel Detectors (GPD) are employed to track photoelectrons. IXPE has a sensitivity much higher than Bragg crystal polarimeters, requiring observational times at least two orders of magnitude smaller than older missions. Another important aspect of IXPE is its ability to provide not only polarization data of the observed source, but also imaging, spectroscopy and timing data at the same time. More details about IXPE and its detectors will be given in Chap. \ref{chap:chapter3}.

\section{Light-matter interaction mechanisms}
There are three main mechanisms with which high energy photons can interact with matter, which are photoelectric effect, Compton scattering and electron-positron pair production. These processes are at the basis of photon detection and are commonly employed in the construction of high-energy photon detectors \cite{longair1992, leo1994}.

\subsection{Photoelectric effect}
When a photon has an energy $E_{\gamma} \ll m_e c^2$ the main interaction process is photoelectric effect, which consists of a photon that is absorbed by an atom with the consequent ejection of a free electron

\begin{equation}
\gamma + (A, Z) \rightarrow e^- + (A, Z)^+.
\end{equation}

A photon with energy $E_{\gamma}$ can be absorbed and cause the ejection of an electron only if $E_{\gamma} \geq E_B$, where $E_B$ is the binding energy of the involved electron. The electron is ejected with a kinetic energy equal to $E_{\gamma} - E_B$.

Atomic electrons are arranged in shells, with increasing binding energy from the outermost to the innermost ones. The atomic energy levels structure can be observed from the appearance of jumps in the photoelectric cross section, which are referred to as absorption edges. Edges appear because when a photon energy exceeds the binding energy of bound electrons in a deeper shell, a new interaction channel opens and the cross section rapidly increases. In heavy elements with high atomic number $Z$, the large binding energy difference between orbitals inside the same shell is responsible for the apperance of further small edges.

The photoelectric cross section usually has a strong dependence on the photon energy and $Z$, and for the K-shell, corresponding to the innermost $1s$ spherical shell, it scales as

\begin{equation}
\sigma_{K} \propto E_{\gamma}^{-3.5}Z^5.
\end{equation}

Outer shells have a strong dependence on energy and atomic number as well, but it gradually becomes softer going outwards. Fig. \ref{fig:photo_cross_section} shows photoelectric cross section dependence on energy for silicon ($Z = 14$) and for the compound material cadmium-telluride (CdTe), a crystal made by cadmium and tellurium ($Z_{Cd} = 48$, $Z_{Te} = 52$), two materials that will be relevant in the following discussion. For both materials is possibe to observe the increases in the cross section corresponding to the K-edge, while for CdTe there is also the L-edge. Because of the large difference between the atomic numbers, CdTe has a photoelectric cross section almost $10^3$ times larger than silicon for energies above the K-edge.

Once the photoelectron has been ejected, the atom is in an excited state and there are two major mechansisms with which it can return to the ground state. Generally, an electron from an outer shell fills the vacancy caused by the photoelectric effect and the difference is in the later de-excitation process.

The first process consists of the emission of an Auger electron, which happens when the electron that filled the vacancy transfers energy to an electron in the outer shells, causing the ejection of the latter with a kinetic energy that corresponds to almost the entire de-excitation energy. Furthermore, the emission direction is isotropic. The second process is fluorescence radiation emission, which happens when the energy is released by emitting a photon with energy corresponding to the binding energy difference between the vacancy and the outer electron levels.

The probability of emitting fluorescence radiation instead of an Auger electron, called fluorescence yield, depends on the atomic number and on the shell. Fluorescence yield generally increases with $Z$ and for inner shells, as shown in Fig. \ref{fig:fluorescence_yield}, and it becomes dominant for atoms from $Z \approx 30$. After one of these de-excitation processes takes place the atom is left with one or two vacancies in the outer shells that, depending on the state of the material, can be filled by recombination, with consequent emission of low energy photons, or by non-radiative thermal dissipation.

Another important property of photoelectric effect is its dependence on the photon polarization. When considering absorption by spherical shells, the differential cross section is \cite{muleri2008}

\begin{equation}
\frac{d \sigma}{d \Omega} \propto \frac{\sin^2 \theta \cos^2 \phi}{(1 - \beta \cos \theta)^4},
\label{eq:polarization_photo}
\end{equation}
where $\phi$ is the angle between the polarization vector and the direction of emission of the photoelectron, and $\theta$ is the polar angle, which measures the angle between the incidence direction of the photon and that of emission of the photoelectron. This dependence can be exploited to measure the degree of polarization of a source, which can be inferred by tracking photoelectrons and studying counts distribution as a function of $\phi$, which is equal to

\begin{equation}
N(\phi) = A + B \cos^2(\phi - \phi_0),
\end{equation}
where $A$ and $B$ are parameteres that depend on the detector response and the degree of polarization of the source. It is possible to define the modulation factor as

\begin{equation}
\mu = \frac{B}{2A + B}.
\end{equation}

In case of 100\% linearly polarized radiation $\mu$ represents the detector response, with 1 indicating a perfect polarimeter and 0 when there is no polarization sensitivity. After the modulation factor of the polarimeter $\mu_{\textrm{inst}}$ is characterized via calibration measurements, the true degree of polarization of the source is calculated from the observations as

\begin{equation}
P = \frac{\mu_{\textrm{meas}}}{\mu_{\textrm{inst}}}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Compton scattering}

Compton scattering is the dominant interaction mechanism at energies around $E_{\gamma} \approx m_ec^2$. This process consists of the interaction between a photon and a free electron, with the photon that transfers part of its energy to the electron. It can be represented as

\begin{equation}
\gamma + e^- \rightarrow \gamma + e^- .
\end{equation}

Given the high energy at which the Compton effect becomes relevant, which is much larger than the energy of the K-edge, the assumption that an atomic electron is free and at rest is a good approximation.

In the frame where the electron is at rest, the photon energy after the interaction is

\begin{equation}
E_{\gamma}' = \frac{E_{\gamma}}{1 + \frac{E_{\gamma}}{m_e c^2}(1 - \cos \theta)},
\end{equation}
where $E_{\gamma}$ is the initial energy and $\theta$ is the angle between the photon initial and final directions. The differential cross section for polarized photons is given by the Klein-Nishina formula

\begin{equation}
\frac{d \sigma_{\textrm{C}}}{d \Omega} = \frac{r_e^2}{2} \left( \frac{E_{\gamma}'}{E_{\gamma}} \right)^2 \left( \frac{E_{\gamma}'}{E} + \frac{E_{\gamma}}{E_{\gamma}'} - 2 \sin^2 \theta \cos^2 \phi \right),
\label{eq:compton}
\end{equation}
where $r_e$ is the classical electron radius, $\phi$ is the azimuthal angle measured with respect to the polarization vector and $\theta$ is the polar angle. When considering an atom with atomic number $Z$, the total Compton cross section scales as $\sigma_{\textrm{tot}} \propto Z \sigma_{\textrm{C}} $, showing a much weaker dependence on $Z$ when compared to photoelectric effect. Fig. \ref{fig:photo_cross_section} shows the total Compton cross sections for silicon and CdTe, compared to other interaction mechanisms cross sections. The interval where the Compton effect is dominant becomes narrower for large atomic numbers. 

As for photoelectric polarimetry, Eq. \ref{eq:compton} is useful to measure polarization for higher energy photons. Indeed, Compton polarimeters are commonly used to study polarization in the hard X-ray and soft $\gamma$-ray bands, in the energy range from a few tens of keV up to a few MeV. 

\subsection{Pair production}
For energies $E_{\gamma} \gg m_ec^2$, approximately above 10 MeV (in the $\gamma$-ray band), the dominant process becomes electron-positron pair production in the nuclear field, represented by

\begin{equation}
\gamma + X \rightarrow X + e^+ + e^- .
\end{equation}

This process can cinematically take place only if the photon has an energy above the threshold $E_{\gamma} \gtrsim 2 m_e c^2$. For an atom with atomic number $Z$, the total cross section scales as 

\begin{equation}
\sigma_{\textrm{pair}} \propto r_e^2 Z^2.
\end{equation}

At low energies, the cross section increases logarithmically with the photon energy, while at high energies it becomes constant, as shown in Fig. \ref{fig:photo_cross_section}.

In high energy astrophysics, this interaction mechanism is exploited in pair production telescopes for $\gamma$-rays, where the produced electron and positron are tracked to determine the incoming photon direction and their energy is measured with an electromagnetic calorimeter to gauge the initial photon energy.

\subsection{Attenuation length}

The total interaction cross section is given by the sum of the cross section of the single processes, as shown in Fig. \ref{fig:photo_cross_section}. When a monochromatic photon beam illuminates a homogeneous material, some photons are absorbed by photoelectric effect or pair production and others are scattered by Compton effect. The result is that the beam is attenuated while it passes through the material, and the variation of the intensity $I$ is described by an exponential decay

\begin{equation}
I(x) = I_0 e^{-x/\mu},
\label{eq:beam_intensity}
\end{equation}
where $I_0$ is the initial intensity and $\mu$ is the mean free path, also called attenuation length, which is related to the total cross section by $\mu = 1 /(\sigma_{tot} \rho)$, thus depends on the energy of the beam and on the material.

Considering a single photon, the absorption probability density function is given by

\begin{equation}
p(x; \mu) = \frac{1}{\mu} e^{-x/\mu},
\label{eq:pdf_photoabs}
\end{equation}
from which it is possible to calculate the photoabsorption efficiency of an absorbing medium with a thickness $d$, which is equal to

\begin{equation}
\varepsilon = \int_0^d \frac{1}{\mu} e^{-x/\mu} \mathrm{d}x = 1 - e^{-d/\mu}.
\end{equation}

Fig. \ref{fig:photoabs} shows how photoelectric absorption efficiency for some materials varies as a function of energy.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{figures/chapter2/photoabs_eff}
    \caption{Dependence of photoelectric absorption efficency on energy for 300 $\mu$m of silicon, 300 $\mu$m of cadmium-telluride (CdTe) and 1 cm of argon (at a temperature of 273 K and pressure of 1 bar). Due to low gas density, argon has a very low efficiency when compared to solid materials like silicon, even if considering a thickness more than ten times larger.}
    \label{fig:photoabs}
\end{figure}

An interesting quantity relevant for later discussion that can be calculated is the mean value $\langle \sqrt{d - x} \rangle$, and in particular seeing how it varies with energy. To calculate this quantity the probability density function to consider is different from Eq. \ref{eq:pdf_photoabs}, because now it is necessary to consider the truncated exponential

\begin{equation}
p(x; \mu) = \frac{e^{-x/\mu}}{\mu(1 - e^{-d/\mu})}, \quad \quad 0 \leq x \leq d.
\label{eq:pdf_truncated}
\end{equation}

Defining $z \equiv \sqrt{d - x}$, the probability density function of $z$ can be found with the change of variable of a probability density function, and is given by

\begin{equation}
p(z; \mu) = \frac{2}{\mu (e^{d/\mu} - 1)} z e^{z^2/\mu}, \quad \quad 0 \leq z \leq \sqrt{d}.
\label{eq:pdf_z}
\end{equation}

The average of this distribution can not be calculated analytically, but can be expressed with special functions, and is equal to

\begin{equation}
\langle z \rangle = \langle \sqrt{d - x} \rangle = \frac{1}{e^{d/\mu} - 1} \left( \sqrt{d} e^{d / \mu} - \frac{\sqrt{\pi \mu}}{2} \operatorname{erfi} (\sqrt{d / \mu}) \right),
\label{eq:z_average}
\end{equation}
where $\operatorname{erfi}(x)$ is the imaginary error function.


\section{Working principles of ionization X-ray detectors}
As said above, photoelectric effect is the dominant interaction process in the X-ray energy band. For this reason, the following discussion is focused on photon detectors that employ this effect to convert an incoming photon into an interpretable signal which allows to measure one or multiple properties among energy, position, time of arrival and polarization.

Various detectors have been devoleped across the years and used on board of X-ray observatories, each of them suitable for measuring one or multiple photon properties in a specific energy interval. The technology employed in the construction of these detectors can vary a lot but all of them share the same working principles to convert photons in a readable signal. First, photons interact with the active material through photoelectric effect and are absorbed, producing a photoelectron. This electron can have a lot of kinetic energy which is then transfered to the active material by collisions. The amount of energy lost by collisions is described by the Bethe-Bloch formula

\begin{equation}
- \frac{dE}{dx} = 2 \pi N_A r_e^2 m_e c^2 \rho \frac{Z}{A} \frac{1}{\beta^2} \left[ \ln \left( \frac{2 m_e \gamma^2 v^2 W_{\textrm{max}}}{I} \right) - 2\beta^2 \right].
\label{eq:bethe_bloch}
\end{equation}

In ionization detectors, the energy transfered by the photoelectron ionizes atoms and molecules releasing charge carriers, which in turn induce a current during their motion. Alternatively, in scintillators molecules are excited by the photoelectron and the signal is represented by the luminous emission produced by the de-excitation which is collected, converted and amplified to generate an electric signal. 

When the photoelectron ionizes the active medium it produces a certain number of charge carriers, which can be electron-ion pairs in gas detectors or electron-hole pairs in semiconductor detectors. The number of charge carriers pairs produced after the interaction with a photon is stochastic and depends on the energy and the gas properties. The mean number of pairs is given by

\begin{equation}
N = \frac{E}{w},
\end{equation}
where $w$ is the mean energy necessary to create a pair and its value is specific to the material. For gases this value ranges between 20 and 40 eV, while for semiconductors it is about an order of magnmitude smaller.

The number of pairs is distributed according to a Poisson distribution, thus the variance is given by the mean number of pairs produced. However, if all the incoming energy is absorbed by the gas, as is the case of X-ray photons, an additional constraint is added, because the energy available to generate charge carriers pairs is fixed by the photon energy. This means that pair production events are not independent, thus the Poisson distribution assumption is no longer valid. Under these hypotheses the variance becomes

\begin{equation}
\sigma^2 = F N,
\label{eq:fano}
\end{equation}
where $F$ is called Fano factor \cite{leo1994}. Tab. \ref{tab:w_fano} shows the values of mean ionization energy and Fano factor for some common materials used for ionization detectors. For the majority of the materials, including gases and semiconductors, $F < 1$. Therefore, when the amount of deposited energy is fixed, the statistics become underdispersed compared to a pure Poissonian process.

The energy resolution of the detector can be estimated with the assumption that if the energy released is sufficiently large the distribution of the number of pairs generated is a Gaussian distribution, with variance obtained from Eq. \ref{eq:fano}. The relative energy resolution at the energy $E$ is defined as
 
\begin{equation}
\frac{\Delta E}{E} = \frac{\operatorname{FWHM}}{E} = 2.35 \frac{\sqrt{F N}}{N} = 2.35 \sqrt{\frac{F w}{E}}.
\label{eq:e_resolution}
\end{equation}

The final energy resolution is the sum of more contributes, as other sources of uncertainties must be taken into account, such as electronic noise and other sources of instrumental noise, and is calculated as

\begin{equation}
\left( \frac{\Delta E}{E} \right)_{\textrm{tot}}^2 = \left( \frac{\Delta E}{E} \right)_{\textrm{Fano}}^2 + \left( \frac{\Delta E}{E} \right)_{\textrm{el. noise}}^2 + \left( \frac{\Delta E}{E} \right)_{\textrm{others}}^2.
\end{equation}

Ionization X-ray detectors can be divided into gas and solid-state detectors. While primarily suited for soft X-rays, they can be adapted for hard X-rays by selecting high-$Z$ materials and optimizing working points. Hard X-ray and soft $\gamma$-ray detection was historically dominated by scintillators, but solid-state detectors made with high-$Z$ materials have increasingly replaced them over the last two decades. The main features of both technologies are discussed below.

\subsection{Gas ionization detectors}
The first X-ray detectors launched into space were gas detectors, thanks to decades of experience and development in ground-based experiments. A gas detector generally consists of a closed chamber filled with pressurized gas. 

In gases, only a few hundreds electron-ion pairs are produced after the interaction of a photon with an energy of a few keV, thus the signal is too small to be detected. Furthermore, without an external field that separates them they would recombine before any signal could be detected. These are the reasons why gas ionization detectors usually have a drift region and a multiplication region, both employing an electric field to separate and accelerate charges.

In the drift region, an electric field of the order of 1 kV/cm is responsible for the separation of primariy electron-ion pairs to avoid recombination. The electric field is generated by applying a potential difference between electrodes. The geometry of electrodes can significantly vary between different detectors, with some examples represented by the axial geometry of cylindrical proportional counters or planar geometry.

Inside the drift region the charges are subjected to diffusion processes due to collisions with gas molecules and thermal agitation. When charges that are initially distributed according to a point-like distribution are free and are not subjected to any external field, they diffuse along each axis and follow a Gaussian distribution that becomes broader with time $t$. The standard deviation of this distribution along an axis is given by

\begin{equation}
\sigma = \sqrt{2D t},
\end{equation}
where $D$ is the diffusion coefficient, which depends on the material and on the working conditions (such as pressure or temperature).

If an electric field is applied along a direction the diffusion time corresponds to the drift time $t_d$, which is determined by the drift distance $x_d$ and the drift velocity $v_d$. The ratio $D/v_d$ can be expressed in terms of the characteristic energy $\epsilon_k$, the charge of the carriers $q$ and the drift electric field intensity $E$, resulting in a diffusion $\sigma_{E}$ equal to

\begin{equation}
\sigma_E = \sqrt{\frac{2Dx_d}{v_d}} = \sqrt{\frac{2\epsilon_k x_d}{qE}}.
\label{eq:diffusion_sigma}
\end{equation}

The minimal dispersion is obtained when the gas is in thermal equilibrium and the characteristic energy is $\epsilon_k = kT$. The diffusion coefficient is different for the transverse and the longitudinal directions and in general it is $D_T > D_L$, meaning that the charge dispersion is broader on the transverse direction. Fig. \ref{fig:diffusion} shows the transverse and longitudinal diffusion widths for different gases and their dependence on the drift field.

    \label{fig:diffusion}
\end{figure}

The multiplication region is where charges are multiplied and it is achieved by accelerating charges in a small region with a strong electric field, with typical peak values of the order of 100 kV/cm. The kinetic energy acquired by the charges is used to start a ionization avalanche, producing new electron-ion pairs that are accelerated by the electric field and in turn continue to ionize the gas. The induced current produced by the charges generates an amplified signal that exceeds electronic noise and can be read and processed. Depending on the voltage applied to the electrodes and the resulting intensity of the electric field, charge multiplication has different working regimes. At low voltages multiplication does not take place and recombination is the dominant process. At higher voltages recombination is not favored, and in a specific voltages interval multiplication occurs and the number of final electron-ion pairs is proportional to the number of primary ionization pairs. The ratio between the initial and final number of pairs represents the gain of the detector.

The proportional regime is essential for spectroscopy, as it is possible to directly measure the photon energy. In this voltage interval, which is called proportional counting region, gain grows exponentially with voltage

\begin{equation}
G = e^{a V}.
\label{eq:exp_gain}
\end{equation}
When the voltage becomes too high the response becomes non-linear and even few primary ionization electrons can produce a large pulse. This regime is called Geiger-Muller region. Detectors working in this regime are able to detect radiation but can not measure the released energy.

The detector that was used in the first dedicated orbital mission, UHURU, consisted in a set of proportional counters. The external part of a proportional counter is usually a thick metallic case, which serves as the cathode, with thin windows of beryllium or plastic materials that are not transparent for optical and UV photons. The anode is a thin metallic wire positioned in the middle and running along the long axis. A voltage is applied to the anode to generate an electric field with an intensity that scales ar $1/r$, where $r$ is the distance from the wire. This dependence allows to use the same electrodes to generate the electric field in the drift zone, in a large region far from the wire, and only when electrons are near the anode they are accelerated to high energies and start producing the avalanche. Due to their small mass, electrons reach the anode too fast, thus is the drift of positive ions to generate a detectable signal. In the case of UHURU, all the proportional counters on board were connected in parallel to a single amplifier, thus it had no intrinsic position resolution. The reasons to have multiple proportional counters were essentially for redundancy, in case some of them would have been damaged, and to implement a veto logic to reject signals from cosmic rays.

Indeed, a thing that can not be neglected in space is that the rate of cosmic rays is much larger than that from all X-ray sources, and they represent a major source of noise that exceeds the relevant signal. In order to suppress it, widely adopted solution is the implementation of anti-coincidence shields, which are sensitive to cosmic rays and transparent for X-rays. An anti-coincidence shield surrounds the detector and allows to reject a signal when radiation penetrates the entire detector.

To mesure the incidence position proportional counters have been gradually improved. Position Sensitive Proportional Counters (PSPC) are one of the improved designs and they have the possibility to reconstruct the incidence position of a photon and its energy. This is achieved by using two separate layers of electrodes as cathodes instead of the metallic case. Each layer is a grid of thin spaced wires oriented orthogonally with respect to the other layer. Using this geometry, one layer gives information on the $x$ coordinate and one on the $y$ coordinate. Usually multiple wires were grouped into virtual strips by connecting them in parallel to a single charge pre-amplifier. The anode is made of parallel wires placed in the middle between the two cathodes. A possible layout is shown in Fig. \ref{fig:pspc}. Position resolution is increased thanks to charge diffusion that takes place inside the gas and spreads the charge over multiple strips on both cathodes. Using a simple charge barycenter algorithm it is possible to achieve sub-strip resolution.

A major step forward in the development of gas detectors was made with Micro-Pattern Gaseous Detectors (MPGDs), which consist of gas-filled detectors with micro-structured readout planes. The innovative aspect of these detectors is that their charge multiplication stages have electrodes spaced by only a few micrometers. This enables better spatial resolution and, thanks to the small distance between the multiplication and collection electrodes, drastically reduces the collection time, allowing the detector to handle higher event rates.

The micro-structures adopted in MPGDs can have different geometries, ranging from one-dimensional to two-dimensional patterns. The first realization was the Micro-Strip Gas Chamber (MSGC) \cite{oed1988}, which consists of an active gas volume enclosed between a drift plane and an insulating substrate. On top of the substrate, alternating thin strips spaced by a few tens of micrometers act as anodes and cathodes, as shown in Fig. \ref{fig:mpgd}.

Another example of an MPGD is the Gas Electron Multiplier (GEM) \cite{sauli1997}, which typically consists of a thin insulating foil (commonly around $50\,\mu\text{m}$ thick, such as Kapton) with a metal coating on both sides, perforated with a dense pattern of micro-holes (usually a few tens of micrometers in diameter). When a voltage difference is applied between the two faces, primary charges drifting near the holes are accelerated by the strong electric field inside them, generating an avalanche that multiplies the initial charge. Unlike MSGCs, a GEM is an independent multiplication stage that must be coupled to readout electrodes to collect the signal. The electric field configuration which forms inside the holes is shown in Fig. \ref{fig:mpgd}.

A two-dimensional concept similar to GEMs with an independent amplification stage is the Micro-Mesh Gaseous Structure (Micromegas) \cite{giomataris1996}. It consists of a thin metallic mesh placed at a very short distance (typically 50--150~$\mu$m) above the readout anode plane. Primary electrons produced in the drift volume pass through the mesh holes and enter this narrow region, where a strong electric field between the mesh and the anode triggers the electron avalanche. An example of Micromegas is shown in Fig. \ref{fig:mpgd}

Despite the developments in gas detectors, X-ray astrophysics has increasingly replaced their use in favor of other detector technologies, such as semiconductor detectors. In recent missions, gas detectors were mainly chosen to cover large detection areas at limited costs compared to other alternatives, or to serve highly specialized purposes, such as X-ray polarimetry.

\subsection{Semiconductor ionization detectors}
\label{sec:semiconductors}

For a long time gas detectors have been the main type of detectors employed on board of high energy astrophysics observatories. One of the negative aspects of these detectors is their limited lifetime due to various instrumental reasons. An example is the presence of consumables like gas, which needs periodical refills due to the unavoidable presence of small leaks in order to maintain to maintain the ideal working conditions. Another issue, which particularly affected traditional gas detectos, is the aging of the electrodes, which can be deteriorated by the polimerization of molecules, with a consequent worsening of the overall performances over the years. An alternative to gas detectors is to use solid state detectors, which do not rely on the use of consumable materials, meaning that ideally the lifetime can be very long. In practice, these detectors are sensitive to other effects, such as radiation damage, but their lifetime is usually much longer than an average gas detector. Currently, there are observatories with solid state detectors that have been operating for more than 25 years, such as Chandra and XMM-Newton.

A class of solid state detectors that have been widely used for soft X-rays observatories is semiconductor ionization detectors. These detectors rely on the same principles of gas ionization detectors, with the difference on the medium employed. Photons interact with an atom via photoelectric effet, produce a photoelectron which in turn ionizes the material and generates electron-hole pairs. A very useful property of semiconductors with respect to gases is that the average energy required to create a pair is small, of the order of some eV, as shown in Tab. \ref{tab:w_fano}. As a consequence, these detectors have an excellent energy resolution. Furthermore, semiconductors have a much higher density than gases, enabling the detection of high energy photons with compact detectors.

A semiconductor detector is, in general, a junction designed to separate the charge carriers generated by radiation interactions and produce a readable electrical signal. A common material for semiconductor detectors is silicon, but there are also other materials such as germanium, gallium arsenide and cadmium telluride. There are different junction types that can be employed to realize a semiconductor detector, depending on the materials and on the required detector properties.

The $pn$ junction  consists of two differently doped semiconductor materials, one with positive charge carriers ($p$) and the other with negative ones ($n$), which are placed in contact to create an interface \cite{kolanoski2020}. The charge gradient which occurs at the boundary generates a diffusion current which leads to charge recombination in a specific region, called depletion zone. Outside of the depletion zone, the doped regions are not neutral and the result is the formation of an electric field that produces a current in the opposite direction to the diffusion current. At equilibrium, a constant voltage difference $V_{bi}$, called built-in voltage, is present and the depletion zone has a width given by

\begin{equation}
d \simeq \sqrt{\frac{2\varepsilon \varepsilon_0}{eN_D} V_{bi}},
\end{equation}
where $\varepsilon$ and $\varepsilon_0$ are the dielectric constant of the material and vacuum, $e$ is the electron charge and $N_D$ is the doping donors concentration. For silicon the typical value of the built-in voltage is 0.6 V. The resulting depletion zone width is of the order of some micrometers and when radiation interacts within this region the produced electron-hole pairs are separated by the electric field and recombination is avoided. The current induced by moving charges is then read and represent the relevant signal. However, due to the very small width of the depletion region, sensitivity to high energy radiation is limited. By applying an external voltage difference $V_{ext}$ to the junction it is possible to extend the depletion zone, thus increasing sensitivity. Specifically, a voltage with the same direction of the built-in voltage (negative at $p$, positive at $n$) must be applied, which is usually called reverse bias. The reverse bias causes a weakening of the diffusion current, thus charge carriers are pushed away from the interface and consequently the depletion zone becomes larger. Under reverse bias, the depletion zone width is given by

\begin{equation}
d_{n,p} \simeq \sqrt{\frac{2\varepsilon \varepsilon_0}{eN_{D, A}} (V_{bi} + V_{ext})},
\label{eq:dep_zone}
\end{equation}
where the subscript $n$ or $p$ indicates the depletion width for the respective doped region. Full depletion is achieved when a sufficiently high $V_{ext}$ is applied and the depletion zone is large as the entire junction.

However, it is not possible to have arbitrarily wide depletion zones by increasing $V_{ext}$, because the electric field becomes so strong that the breakdown point is reached and the junction breaks. A solution is to decrease the dopants concentration, so that with the same voltage a larger region is depleted. This requires to produce very high pure crystals and some examples are represented by High-Purity Germanium (HPGe) or High-Resistivity Silicon (HR-Si) detectors, which can reach depletion widths of the order of a few centimeters and a few hundreds of micrometers, respectively.

In the past it was technologically impossible to achieve such high purity levels in crystal realization processes, thus an alternative to obtain depletion zones of the order of millimeters or centimeters was to dope semiconductors in order to have the same concentration of donors and acceptors. These materials are called compensated materials, and they act as if they were pure. By placing the compensated material, indicated with the letter $i$, between a $p$-doped and a $n$-doped region, a $pin$ junction is formed, which have similar results to high-purity detectors. An example of $pin$ junctions are Si(Li) or Ge(Li), where lithium is used to compensate semiconductors. The first semiconductor detector to be employed on board of an X-ray observatory, the Einstein Observatory, was a $pin$ junction detector used for spectroscopy. This detector was able to achieve an energy resolution better than all the previous gas detectors. The negative aspect of this detector was that it was made by a single piece of silicon, thus it was not possible to carry out high resolution imaging and spectroscopy at the same time. An alternative that was later developed to also perform imaging are charge-coupled devices (CCDs), which have a substrate divided into a grid of small pixels. Charges are produced when a photon interacts with a pixel and they are stored inside it, because it acts as a potential well. Then charges are transferred serially between ajacent pixels towards an external circuit responsible for the readout.

Another type of junction is the Schottky. This junction is obtained at the contact interface between a semiconductor and a metal which have a difference between the vacuum energy and the Fermi energy larger than that of the semiconductor. At thermal equilibrium semiconductor electrons flow towards the metal, a depletion zone is formed on the interface and a potential barrier is established. As for $pn$ junctions, when operating in reverse bias by applying negative voltage to the metal, the depletion zone becomes larger because the external electric field pulls electrons far from the semiconductor. The width of the depletion zone still follows Eq. \ref{eq:dep_zone}. This junction is useful for compound materials, where it is difficult to grow crystals with few impurities or crystal imperfections, posing a technical limit to $N_D$ and resulting in a very high voltage required to achieve a large depletion zone. Some examples of common materials for which a Schottky junction is ideal are GaAs or CdTe and CdZnTe, which are all high-$Z$ materials with a large interaction cross section for high energy photons in the hard X-ray and $\gamma$-ray regions.

Finally, another important junction is obtained with a layer of semiconductor, one of oxide and one of metal, forming a Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor (MOS) junction. When a voltage below a specific thershold is applied a depletion zone is formed, but its width is usually smaller than what it is possible to obtain with other junctions. MOS are commonly employed in CCDs, but due to the small depletion zone they are typically suited for energies below 10 keV and loose efficient for higher energies. $pn$CCDs, which were developed later, allow to obtain larger depletion zones and thus to increase sensitivity to high energy photons up to a few tens of keV. Overall, CCDs allowed to obtain many progresses in X-ray astrophysics because it became possible to achieve high resolution for both imaging and spectroscopy at the same time as well as an higher quantum efficiency with respect to gas detectors.

As mentioned above, CCDs work by moving the accumulated charge inside each pixel towards a readout circuit to digitalize the signals. On a large CCD, which can have up to thousands of pixels per side, this process can take from tens of milliseconds up to a few seconds to read the entire array. Due to the absence of a shutter on X-ray telescopes, while charges are moving the CCD is still exposed to radiation and image deterioration can occur. A solution is to move the frame in auxiliars arrays which are not exposed, enabling to reduce this effect. However, to correctly perform spectroscopy it is necessary that event pile-up does not occur, but this is limited by readout speed. The consequence of this problem is that it is difficult to perform spectroscopy of bright sources with rates much larger than the readout rate.

To overcome the readout speed limitations and pile-up issues of CCDs while preserving high energy resolution, Silicon Drift Detectors (SDDs) were developed. An SDD is built on a high-resistivity silicon substrate with reverse-biased $pn$ junctions on its surfaces that shape an internal electric field to guide primary electrons laterally toward a microscopic $n$ collecting anode. By decoupling the anode size from the total active area, the readout capacitance is kept small, which drastically reduces electronic noise. Consequently, SDDs achieve an energy resolution close to the Fano limit and can process high count rates exceeding $10^5$ counts/s per channel with microsecond time resolution and minimal pile-up. Their primary trade-off compared to CCDs is a lower intrinsic spatial resolution. A comparison between the design of a CCD and a SDD is shown in Fig. \ref{fig:ccd_sdd}

SDDs have gained increasing importance in X-ray astrophysics, as they achieve high timing and energy resolution simultaneously. They are currently employed in operating missions like NICER and are planned for next-generation large X-ray observatories, such as eXTP \cite{zhang2019}.

A different approach to CCDs and SDDs is to separate the active part of the detector, where the interaction between radiation and matter takes place and electron-hole pairs are generated, from the passive readout stage. Detectors that adopt this approach are called Hybrid Pixel Detectors (HPDs), in contrast to monolithic detectors like CCDs. This design allows for the construction of highly-optimized detectors, where the readout chip can be designed to satisfy the requirements needed for the specific application without the contraints imposed by the active sensor.  

As in gas detectors, charge carriers drifting inside the depleted semiconductor volume undergo thermal diffusion due to collisions with the crystal lattice. The general principles described for gases remain valid, with the key difference that in low-field conditions charge carrier transport in semiconductors obeys the Einstein relation

\begin{equation}
D = \mu_c \frac{k T}{e},
\end{equation}
where $D$ is the diffusion coefficient, $\mu_c$ is the charge carrier mobility, $k$ is the Boltzmann constant, $T$ is the absolute temperature, and $e$ is the elementary charge. 

Considering that the drift time $t_d$ required to traverse a depletion width $d$ under a uniform electric field $\mathcal{E}$ is $t_d = d / v_d = d / (\mu_c \mathcal{E})$, Eq. \ref{eq:diffusion_sigma} can be rewritten for semiconductors as

\begin{equation}
\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{2 D d}{\mu_c \mathcal{E}}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 k T}{e \mathcal{E}} d} = \mathcal{D} \sqrt{d},
\label{eq:diff_sigma_dist}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{D} \equiv \sqrt{2 k T / (e \mathcal{E})}$ is a parameter depending only on the operational temperature and internal electric field.

This thermal diffusion sets a physical limit on the minimum size of the charge cloud reaching the electrodes. In pixelated or strip detectors, this spatial spread causes charge sharing among neighboring elements, which can either reduce the single-pixel signal-to-noise ratio or be exploited using algorithms to achieve sub-pixel spatial resolution.
